# Memetics Map (Concept Map + Characterization Passport)

- Date: 2026-02-05 [S03]
- Valid-until: 2026-08-05 (refresh triggers: major new synthesis/meta-analysis; materially new data access for diffusion platforms; major definitional shifts in the scholarly framing). [S01]
- Scope: research framing and measurement options for “memetics” as a candidate framework for cultural evolution; **no manipulation playbooks**. [S01] [S03]

## 0) How to read this map

This is a **set-valued** map: it keeps multiple competing definitions and framings alive when they trade off conceptual clarity vs empirical tractability. (No silent scalarization.) [S01] [S02]

### The three required framings (parity set)
1. **Memetics-as-replicators:** cultural “memes” treated as replicating units in a Darwin-analogy. [S05] [S28]
2. **Dual-inheritance / cultural evolution (population thinking):** cultural change as Darwinian evolution in socially learned information, without requiring gene-like discrete units. [S14] [S05]
3. **Epidemiology of representations / cultural attraction:** culture as distributions of representations shaped by cognitive/social transformation processes (“attraction”), not just copying. [S18] [S22] [S28]

## 1) Core terms (with competing definitions)

### 1.1 “Memetics” (label for a family of programs; contested)
- Supported: “Memetics” is presented (in this sprint’s core pack) as one possible Darwin-inspired approach to culture, alongside other Darwin-inspired approaches (e.g., dual-inheritance; epidemiology of representations). [S28]
- Contested: Whether “memetics” provides a distinct, scientifically useful research program depends on how strongly “meme” is defined (strong replicator claim vs weak commonplace re-labeling). [S28]

### 1.2 “Meme” (candidate definitions; do not collapse)
Define a **portfolio** of “meme” constructs; treat each as a different hypothesis about what is preserved/transmitted.

Candidate definitions:
- **Meme-A (strong replicator claim):** “meme” is a culturally transmitted item that is *reproduced* in a way analogous to biological replication; this is a substantive empirical claim, not a definitional truth. [S28] [S05]
- **Meme-B (weak / unit-of-imitation gloss):** “meme” as a unit of cultural transmission/imitation; risks collapsing into a near-synonym for ordinary “cultural item” unless further operational commitments are added. [S06] [S28]
- **Meme-C (operational proxy in online data):** “meme” operationalized as an observable token family (e.g., hashtags) within a platform-specific transmission channel (e.g., retweets). This is a measurement choice, not an ontological claim. [S24]

Notes:
- Contested: whether cultural “units” are discrete and copy-like enough to support Meme-A in many domains; transformation during acquisition is a central objection. [S28] [S29] [S22]

### 1.3 “Cultural evolution” (definition used in this repo)
- Supported (definition): cultural evolution as Darwinian change in socially acquired information (e.g., knowledge, beliefs), not restricted to gene-like units. [S14]
- Supported: memetics debates can reveal misunderstandings about Darwinian approaches to cultural evolution, including overly literal gene↔culture analogies. [S05]

### 1.4 “Representation” (epidemiology framing)
- Supported: explaining cultural phenomena as developing an “epidemiology of representations” (culture modeled as distributions of representations, and processes that shape them). [S18]
- Supported: culture is better treated as graded/continuous (no sharp demarcation between individual vs cultural information), which pushes against overly discrete-unit pictures. [S29]

### 1.5 “Cultural attractor” (attraction / transformative transmission)
- Supported (one disambiguation): cultural attractors are theoretical posits capturing why some variants are more likely outcomes of transformations than others. [S22]
- Supported: ambiguity in the “cultural attractor” concept can undermine explanation if not disambiguated. [S22]

## 2) Mechanism map (what processes each framing emphasizes)

### 2.1 Transmission: preservative vs constructive (key axis)
- Supported: critiques emphasize that cultural items are often **re-produced** (constructed/transformed) rather than straightforwardly copied, challenging strong replicator assumptions. [S28] [S29] [S22]
- Supported: in online behavior adoption, reinforcement from multiple sources can be required (complex contagion), implying that a “copy-once” picture can be empirically inadequate for some behaviors. [S26]

### 2.2 Selection vs attraction vs “attention economics” (key axis)
- Supported: cultural evolution overviews frame cultural change as Darwinian at a high level, but emphasize differences from genetic evolution (including transmission pathways and sources). [S14]
- Supported: limited attention can be modeled as a constraint shaping which online “memes” persist/spread, using hashtags as operational proxies. [S24]
- Contested/unknown: to what extent “selection” language is appropriate in a given domain versus “attraction” (transformative convergence) depends on operational definitions and measurement. [S05] [S22] [S28]

## 3) Framing passports (commitments, strengths, critiques, falsifiers)

This section is not “who is right?”; it lists what each framing *must* make true (or at least plausible) to earn its keep. [S02]

### 3.1 Framing 1: Memetics-as-replicators
- Ontic commitment (strong form; contested): there exist meme-like cultural units that are reproduced with sufficient fidelity for selection-like dynamics to be explanatory. [S28] [S05]
- Typical operational unit choices (often under-specified; contested): discrete items (phrases, tunes, practices) treated as “the same meme” across hosts/contexts. [S28] [S29]
- Strengths (conditional): when high-fidelity copying and stable unit identity hold, Darwin-analogy modeling may yield clear predictions and measurements. (Inference; depends on fidelity/unit-identity being measurable.) [S28]
- Core critiques:
  - Strong “meme” definition is a substantive empirical claim; weak definition becomes near-trivial. [S28]
  - Many cultural transmissions are transformative/construction-heavy, challenging strong replicator assumptions. [S28] [S29] [S22]
- Falsifiers / failure modes (as test templates; inference but anchored):
  - If repeated transmission shows systematic reconstruction toward “attractors” rather than faithful copying, strong replicator picture is weakened for that domain. [S22] [S28] [S29]
  - If unit identity cannot be specified without ad hoc choices (variant boundaries unstable across coders/contexts), replicator-unit modeling loses explanatory specificity. (Inference; grounded in the “weak vs strong meme” critique.) [S28]

### 3.2 Framing 2: Dual-inheritance / cultural evolution (population thinking)
- Ontic commitment: socially learned information varies and changes over time in ways that can be studied with evolutionary tools, without requiring gene-like discrete units. [S14] [S05]
- Emphasis: population-level distributions and change; explicit modeling of how social learning produces change (often via biases, structure, and transmission pathways). [S14]
- Strengths:
  - Offers a broadly Darwinian framing while avoiding overcommitment to “meme as replicator” discreteness. [S14] [S05]
- Core critiques / boundary risks:
  - “Darwinian” can be misread as requiring too-literal gene-analogy; memetics debates show the need for careful specification of mechanisms and units. [S05]
- Falsifiers / failure modes (as test templates; inference but anchored):
  - If observed cultural change is dominated by transformation processes that systematically overwrite inherited content (rather than differential retention of variants), models that assume mostly preservative transmission may misfit. [S22] [S29]
  - If “socially learned information” cannot be operationalized for the domain (measurement failure), the framing becomes underdetermined. (Inference; the definition is broad by design.) [S14]

### 3.3 Framing 3: Epidemiology of representations / cultural attraction
- Ontic commitment: culture is best explained as distributions of representations shaped by cognitive and social processes (analogous to epidemiology), with “attraction” capturing convergent transformation. [S18] [S22]
- Emphasis: microfoundations in cognitive psychology; mechanisms of reconstruction/transformation and why distributions stabilize. [S18] [S22]
- Strengths:
  - Directly models transformative transmission and graded culturalness, aligning with critiques of overly preservative copying pictures. [S29] [S22] [S28]
- Core critiques / boundary risks:
  - “Attractor” concepts require careful disambiguation to avoid explanatory vagueness. [S22]
- Falsifiers / failure modes (as test templates; inference but anchored):
  - If transmission is empirically mostly preservative with high fidelity and stable unit identity, attraction-centric explanations may be unnecessary or dominated by simpler preservative models for that domain. (Inference; framed as a possible dominance relation.) [S22] [S28]
  - If cognitive microfoundations are not measurable (or not needed) for prediction in a domain (e.g., purely statistical diffusion fits), then epidemiology framing may be less tractable for that domain. (Inference; compare with attention-limited diffusion modeling.) [S24] [S18]

### 5.4 Comparability rules (no silent scalarization)
- **Same window/budget rule:** parity comparisons must fix data windows, tool budgets, and procedures before results. [S01]
- **No cross-type aggregation:** do not average ordinal indicators as if interval; publish a Pareto set if trade-offs remain. [S01] [S02]
- **Proxy discipline:** treat operational proxies (e.g., hashtags) as context-bound measurement choices; do not reify them as the underlying cultural objects. [S24]
