# Evidence Pack (Memetics Research Sprint)

## Purpose
Collect an auditable set of primary / peer-reviewed / canonical sources about memetics and competing cultural-evolution framings, with short extracted quotes (≤25 words/source) and explicit notes about what each source supports, contests, or leaves uncertain. [S01] [S03]

## Conventions
- Citation format: `[S##]` (see `work/reading_list.md`, ADR-000).
- Quote policy: ≤25 words per source (short excerpt only). [S01]
- Status labels used below:
  - **Supported:** the source directly argues/defines/reports this.
  - **Contested:** credible sources disagree or treat as unclear.
  - **Unknown:** not established in the current pack; requires more evidence.

## Source notes (core pack)

### S05 — Henrich, Boyd, Richerson (2008) — Five misunderstandings about cultural evolution
- Why it matters:
  - Directly addresses “memes / replicators” debates and clarifies what does and does not follow for cultural-evolution modeling. [S05]
- Extracted quote (≤25 words):
  - “Recent debates about memetics have revealed some widespread misunderstandings about Darwinian approaches to cultural evolution.” [S05]
- Key takeaways (with status):
  - Supported: The paper frames memetics debates as revealing common misunderstandings about Darwinian approaches to culture. [S05]
  - Supported: It explicitly names “memes”, “replicators”, “dual inheritance theory”, and “epidemiology of representations” as keywords and targets claims about discreteness, replicators, and bias. [S05]
  - Supported: It argues both “memeticists” and “their critics” can be misled by an overly enthusiastic genes↔culture analogy, while still treating cultural evolution as Darwinian in a general sense. [S05]

### S06 — RichardDawkins.net (2014) — What’s in a Meme? (archived capture)
- Why it matters:
  - Provides a public-facing synthesis of the “meme” concept, including Dawkins’s initial definition (quoted) and discussion of academic controversy. [S06]
- Extracted quote (≤25 words):
  - “conveys the idea of a unit of cultural transmission, or a unit of imitation” [S06]
- Key takeaways (with status):
  - Supported: The article reports that “the meme first appeared” in *The Selfish Gene* (1976) and links the meme concept to explaining spread/persistence of ideas/behaviors. [S06]
  - Supported: It states memetics is controversial in academic circles and warns against oversimplifying complex phenomena into simple models without strong caveats. [S06]
  - Contested: The piece is not peer-reviewed and contains broad claims about “serious scholars” and reception; treat as orientation and use peer-reviewed sources for adjudication. [S06] [S05]

### S14 — Mesoudi (2018) — Cultural evolution (eLS encyclopedia entry)
- Why it matters:
  - A compact, expert overview of cultural evolution as a Darwinian process, including definitions, mechanisms, and differences from genetic evolution. [S14]
- Extracted quote (≤25 words):
  - “Cultural evolution is the idea that cultural change, that is changes in socially acquired information such as knowledge or beliefs, constitutes a Darwinian evolutionary process” [S14]
- Key takeaways (with status):
  - Supported: Defines cultural evolution as Darwinian change in socially acquired information (not limited to “gene-like” units). [S14]
  - Supported: Explicitly distinguishes similarities and differences between genetic and cultural evolution (e.g., transmission pathways and sources). [S14]
  - Supported: Identifies key methodological pillars used in the field (models, experiments/fieldwork, phylogenetics), and points to classic dual-inheritance sources. [S14]

### S18 — Sperber (1985) — Toward an epidemiology of representations
- Why it matters:
  - Canonical statement of the “epidemiology of representations” framing: culture as distributions of representations, requiring cognitive-psychology grounding. [S18]
- Extracted quote (≤25 words):
  - “To explain cultural phenomena is, then, to develop an epidemiology of representations.” [S18]
- Key takeaways (with status):
  - Supported: Proposes explaining cultural phenomena via an “epidemiology of representations” rooted in cognitive psychology (analogy to pathology↔epidemiology). [S18]
  - Supported: Treats cultural things as distributions of representations and emphasizes the processes that generate and stabilize those distributions. [S18]

### S22 — Buskell (2017) — What are cultural attractors?
- Why it matters:
  - Clarifies and disambiguates “cultural attractor” usage; connects the concept to transformative (constructive) transmission. [S22]
- Extracted quote (≤25 words):
  - “Cultural attractors are theoretical posits that capture the way in which certain ideational variants are more likely to be the outcome of transformations than others.” [S22]
- Key takeaways (with status):
  - Supported: Presents “cultural attractor theory” as a transformative approach where variants are transformed during acquisition (contrasted with preservative copying). [S22]
  - Supported: Identifies multiple distinct conceptualizations of “cultural attractors” and argues ambiguity here causes problems in theorizing. [S22]

### S24 — Weng et al. (2012) — Competition among memes with limited attention
- Why it matters:
  - Provides a measurable operational proxy for “memes” (hashtags) and a model where limited attention + network structure yields heavy-tailed meme popularity. [S24]
- Extracted quote (≤25 words):
  - “The wide adoption of social media has increased the competition among ideas for our finite attention.” [S24]
- Key takeaways (with status):
  - Supported: Uses Twitter hashtags as operational proxies for memes and retweets as a transmission mechanism. [S24]
  - Supported: Claims (in abstract) that popularity/persistence heterogeneity can be explained via limited attention + network structure “without the need to assume different intrinsic values among ideas.” [S24]
  - Caution: The “hashtag=meme” proxy is context-bound and resolution-dependent; treat as an operationalization, not a definition. [S24]

### S26 — Centola (2010) — Complex contagion and reinforcement (online experiment manuscript)
- Why it matters:
  - Empirical support for “complex contagion” where adoption often needs reinforcement from multiple sources; relevant to modeling cultural diffusion beyond simple replicator copying. [S26]
- Extracted quote (≤25 words):
  - “social behavior is a complex contagion: people usually require contact with multiple sources of ‘infection’ before being convinced to adopt a behavior” [S26]
- Key takeaways (with status):
  - Supported: Contrasts simple contagion assumptions with complex contagion and tests network topology effects experimentally. [S26]
  - Supported: Reports that multiple-neighbor reinforcement increases adoption likelihood and can change diffusion outcomes across network structures. [S26]

### S28 — Sperber (2000) — Objection to the memetic approach to culture
- Why it matters:
  - Direct critique of memetics-as-strong-replicator claim; explicitly situates memetics among alternative Darwin-inspired approaches (incl. dual-inheritance and epidemiology). [S28]
- Extracted quote (≤25 words):
  - “Memetics is one possible evolutionary approach to the study of culture.” [S28]
- Key takeaways (with status):
  - Supported: Explicitly names Boyd & Richerson-style models and “epidemiology of representations” as alternative Darwin-inspired approaches alongside memetics. [S28]
  - Supported: Argues that if “meme” is taken in a strong Dawkins sense, it is a substantive empirical claim; if defined weakly (OED-style), it becomes a near-rewording of a commonplace view of culture. [S28]
  - Supported: Develops an objection focused on “re-produced” vs “reproduced/copied” cultural items (transformation vs copying). [S28]

### S29 — Sperber & Claidière (2008) — Defining and explaining culture
- Why it matters:
  - Sharpens definitional boundaries: culture as a graded property (not a discrete type), and transmission as a mix of preservative + constructive processes. [S29]
- Extracted quote (≤25 words):
  - “there is a continuum of cases without any demarcation between more individual and more cultural information” [S29]
- Key takeaways (with status):
  - Supported: Argues there is a continuum between more individual and more cultural information; therefore culture is better treated as a property with degrees. [S29]
  - Supported: Emphasizes both preservative and constructive processes in transmission, and critiques overly preservative (copy-like) pictures. [S29]

### S30 — Aral, Muchnik, Sundararajan (2009) — Influence vs homophily in diffusion
- Why it matters:
  - Provides a quantitative correction for “peer influence” estimates by separating influence from homophily, a major confound in diffusion claims. [S30]
- Extracted quote (≤25 words):
  - “previous methods overestimated peer influence effects by 300% to 700% and that homophily explains more than 50%” [S30]
- Key takeaways (with status):
  - Supported: The paper reports substantial overestimation of peer influence when homophily is not modeled. [S30]
  - Supported: It provides a method to separate influence from homophily in dynamic networks, changing diffusion effect sizes. [S30]

### S31 — Ugander et al. (2012) — Structural diversity in social contagion
- Why it matters:
  - Shows that adoption probability depends on exposure across multiple network components, not just the count of exposures. [S31]
- Extracted quote (≤25 words):
  - “the probability of contagion is tightly controlled by the number of connected components in an individual’s contact neighborhood” [S31]
- Key takeaways (with status):
  - Supported: Structural diversity (distinct social circles) predicts adoption better than raw exposure count. [S31]
  - Supported: This supports “complex contagion” mechanisms needing reinforcement from separate sources. [S31]

### S32 — Romero, Meeder, Kleinberg (2011) — Topic-dependent diffusion mechanics
- Why it matters:
  - Empirically distinguishes how different topics (idioms vs hashtags) diffuse, useful for operational proxy sensitivity in memetics. [S32]
- Extracted quote (≤25 words):
  - “We find significant variation in the ways that widely-used hashtags on different topics spread.” [S32]
- Key takeaways (with status):
  - Supported: Diffusion patterns vary systematically by topic, not just by network structure. [S32]
  - Supported: The paper links some topics to complex-contagion-like spreading, challenging uniform “simple contagion” assumptions. [S32]

### S33 — Granovetter (1978) — Threshold models of collective behavior
- Why it matters:
  - Foundational definition of “threshold” in collective behavior; underpins complex-contagion reinforcement logic. [S33]
- Extracted quote (≤25 words):
  - “The key concept is that of ‘threshold’: the number or proportion of others who must make one decision before a given actor does so.” [S33]
- Key takeaways (with status):
  - Supported: Collective behavior can be modeled with heterogeneous thresholds that depend on others’ choices. [S33]
  - Supported: Different threshold distributions can yield very different aggregate outcomes even with similar average preferences. [S33]

### S34 — Watts (2002) — Global cascades with threshold rules
- Why it matters:
  - Formalizes cascade dynamics on random networks under a threshold rule; relevant to predicting large-scale diffusion. [S34]
- Extracted quote (≤25 words):
  - “decisions are determined by the actions of their neighbors according to a simple threshold rule.” [S34]
- Key takeaways (with status):
  - Supported: The model links rare, large cascades to network structure plus threshold-based decision rules. [S34]
  - Supported: Identifies network regimes where very large cascades are possible but rare. [S34]

### S35 — Guilbeault, Becker, Centola (2017) — Complex contagions: decade in review
- Why it matters:
  - Review of complex contagion research across domains; summarizes key mechanisms and modeling directions. [S35]
- Extracted quote (≤25 words):
  - “complex contagions have been studied across an enormous variety of social domains.” [S35]
- Key takeaways (with status):
  - Supported: Surveys empirical domains (health, innovation, politics, social media) where complex contagions are studied. [S35]
  - Supported: Highlights network topology and individual thresholds as central theoretical themes. [S35]

### S36 — Reisinger et al. (2024) — Patterns of stability in complex contagions
- Why it matters:
  - Recent open-access method paper linking complex contagion to network component analysis and stability. [S36]
- Extracted quote (≤25 words):
  - “Diseases typically propagate through single connections, while behaviors and beliefs often necessitate multiple connections for further propagation.” [S36]
- Key takeaways (with status):
  - Supported: Distinguishes simple vs complex contagions by single vs multiple-connection requirements. [S36]
  - Supported: Proposes a graph-reduction method to identify components relevant to complex contagion spread. [S36]
